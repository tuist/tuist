At Tuist we are obssessed with helping everyone build fast. Part of that responsibility lays in fast inference these days, but inference has done its job, it might get verified by building it, and this is where we have a role to play. 


<!--
Every build cache we've seen starts in the same place: the artifacts go in object storage, something warm sits in front of it, and the warm thing goes as close to the builders as you can afford. Ours did too, and for years it was the part of the product we thought about least.

Then several things arrived at once. Apple shipped compilation caching in Xcode 26 and the traffic showing up at our door inverted. The object storage underneath us started failing at our volume, and those failures reached developers as network errors in the middle of a build that we could neither explain nor fix. For a company whose product is build speed, having no better answer than a shrug is about as bad as it gets. Since last November we've thrown that service away and rebuilt it as roughly seven times as much code. Most of what follows is the engineering. Some of it is the months we spent fixing symptoms one at a time without noticing they were the same problem.

A build cache spends almost all of its requests on artifacts a few kilobytes long and almost all of its bytes on a handful of enormous ones. On our own fleet, half of every cache read we serve is 16 KB or smaller and three quarters are under 64 KB, while the artifacts at the other end run past 256 MB. **Those aren't the same engineering problem.** For small artifacts the challenge is latency: the payload costs nothing to move, so what costs you is the round trip, and a build makes thousands of them. At 40ms each, ten thousand lookups is close to seven minutes of a build spent waiting on a network doing nothing but saying yes. For large artifacts the challenge is bandwidth: a round trip is noise against a 100MB framework, and what costs you is how fast the bytes move, and whether a transfer that dies at 90% has to start again from zero. Latency wants the data sitting next to the client and the protocol collapsed into as few round trips as you can manage. Bandwidth wants the bytes to never touch your application at all. Optimize a single path for both and you usually end up mediocre at each.

A cache is also an economic argument before it's a technical one: if serving a hit costs you more than recomputing the thing would have, you've built an expensive way to be slow. There's an easier version of this problem that settles the physics and the economics in one move, and it's where most of the industry has landed. If you own the compute as well as the cache, you can put them on the same private network, the distance to the data becomes a millisecond, the bandwidth becomes whatever the switch will do, and most of what makes build system caching hard stops being your problem. It also happens to be the version with the tidiest business model. When the build runs on your machines you can bill it by the minute, and the cache stops being a product with its own economics and becomes a feature that makes your compute look fast. It's the carmaker building its own charging network: excellent chargers, sited where that company's cars need them, and no use to you at all if you bought a different car. We run [Tuist Runners](https://tuist.dev/en/docs/guides/features/runners) ourselves and compute and cache do sit on the same private network there. But **every hour you spend making the cache faster inside your own environment only pays off for builds you're already being paid for.**

Most of the time a developer spends waiting on a build, they aren't on CI. They're on a laptop, on whatever connection they happen to have, possibly an ocean away from wherever you decided to put the cache. And increasingly the build isn't running on a machine anybody at the company chose at all: coding agents bring their own sandboxes, spun up wherever their provider happens to run them and torn down when the task finishes, and that environment is not yours to place, to warm, or to collocate anything with. If a cache is only fast when it's sitting next to your runners, everyone outside that perimeter gets a fraction of the benefit, and sometimes a regression. We came at this from the other direction, mostly by accident of history. We started with the cache years before we had runners, so the instinct was to make it fast everywhere rather than fast in the one environment we fully controlled. The payoff there isn't only ours. When build speed stops depending on whose compute you're renting, it stops being something a team buys and becomes something the ecosystem has: the same cache at the same speed for a developer on a laptop, an open source project with no CI budget at all, and a company running thousands of builds a day on managed infrastructure. That constraint shaped most of what follows.

## It started with the module cache

The [module cache](https://tuist.dev/en/docs/guides/features/cache/module-cache) came out of [project generation](https://tuist.dev/en/docs/guides/features/projects). Because Tuist generates the Xcode project, it knows the dependency graph, and knowing the graph is what lets you work out which modules haven't changed, swap them for precompiled binaries, and generate a project that skips compiling them entirely. What comes out the other side is a particular kind of artifact: whole compiled modules, frameworks, the output of real compilation work. Across five million module cache downloads, the median artifact a client pulls sits between 256 KB and 512 KB, a tenth of them are above 4 MB, one in a hundred is above 32 MB, and the last fraction of a percent runs past 256 MB. Which meant that from the first day our performance profile was bandwidth-bound. One lookup, then a large transfer, and the round trip barely registering against the size of what we were moving.

So we put them in object storage, which is what most teams reach for and what most teams should reach for. It's cheap, it's durable, it's somebody else's operational problem, and it moves large objects perfectly well. For a long stretch it did what we needed. What we hadn't priced in was what happens to a managed object store when you point real build traffic at it, and we found out faster than we expected. At our volumes of uploads and downloads, every provider we tried started producing failures, and because those failures happened between the client and the bucket, they surfaced to developers as network errors in the middle of a build with nothing on our side to point at and nothing we could do to fix them. We switched providers. It happened again. Being unable to answer "why did my build fail" with anything better than a shrug is not a position you can stay in, and once we'd seen the same shape of problem from more than one vendor it stopped looking like a bad provider and started looking like the architecture.

The physics were pushing in the same direction. The assumption that comes bundled with reaching for object storage, usually without anyone stopping to examine it, is that it will keep being fine for any artifact size. It won't, and we wrote up the reasoning in [The physics of build systems](/blog/2026/08/25/the-physics-of-build-systems): a cache hit costs roughly `R × L + S / B`, where R is the number of round trips you can't overlap, L is latency, S is the bytes you have to move, and B is the bandwidth you have to move them through. Which term dominates depends entirely on the shape of your artifacts. Large ones put you squarely in `S / B`, where object storage does fine and the distance to the bucket is an implementation detail. Small ones put you in `R × L`, where the distance is the entire cost and no amount of cheap durable storage will save you. None of this made our original decision wrong. It was a correct answer to the workload we had. **What changed underneath us is the workload.**

## Then the artifacts got small

Xcode 26 introduced compilation caching built on LLVM's content-addressable storage, at sub-function granularity, and [we plugged into it](/blog/2025/10/22/xcode-cache) as soon as it shipped. It is a completely different shape of traffic arriving at the same door. Not one transfer of a framework but thousands of tiny lookups, a great many of them doing nothing more interesting than asking whether something exists. The median artifact the Xcode CAS serves is between 8 KB and 16 KB, and its 99th percentile is under a megabyte, which is roughly where the module cache's distribution begins. It also arrives four times as often: twenty-one million of those lookups against five million module cache downloads over the same period. Same infrastructure, inverted profile. Every property that made object storage a good fit for the module cache stopped mattering, and the one property we'd never had to care about, the distance to the data, became the only one that did. The split is easiest to see on the fleet still carrying most of our traffic, where both workloads run side by side on the same nodes. The Xcode CAS accounts for **80% of the requests and 6% of the bytes**. The module cache accounts for 20% of the requests and 94% of the bytes, at an average of 3.8 MB a download against the CAS's 60 KB. Almost every request is small and almost every byte is in a large one, and the same system has to be good at both.

Bazel and Buck2 were on the roadmap too, and they push in the same direction. A remote execution cache is thousands of small action lookups and blob fetches, so the operation count climbs and the artifact size falls again, and everything we'd learned about being bandwidth-bound stops applying. That is also where the economics started to come apart. Object storage bills you per operation as well as per byte, and a handful of operations costs nothing, but a build doing tens of thousands of PUTs and GETs is a different invoice entirely. The part that made it hard to plan around is that the operation count isn't ours to control. It falls out of how a team has structured their project: a finely grained graph with many small modules produces far more and far smaller artifacts than a coarse one, so two accounts on the same plan could land different costs on us, and the account with the better-factored project was the expensive one. Reliability we couldn't explain, physics moving against us, and a cost curve driven by somebody else's dependency graph. It was clear we had to do something.


## The cache in front of the cache

Our first answer, built between November 2025 and the spring, was a cache service written in [Elixir](https://elixir-lang.org/). It ran as a [Phoenix](https://www.phoenixframework.org/) application, one instance per host, on ten cloud VMs spread across eight regions. The shape was a warm cache in front of durable storage: each node had a volume mounted at `/cas`, [nginx](https://nginx.org/) sat in front and served artifact bytes off that volume through a zero-copy `sendfile` path with kernel TLS, and object storage held the authoritative copy. Phoenix never touched the bytes on a read. It answered an `auth_request` subrequest, handed nginx an `X-Accel-Redirect`, and got out of the way. Each node tracked artifact access metadata in [SQLite](https://sqlite.org/) so eviction could be least-recently-used rather than random, [Cachex](https://github.com/whitfin/cachex) held the key-value store and auth results, and a fleet of [Oban](https://github.com/oban-bg/oban) workers handled eviction, orphan cleanup, and batched transfers to and from the bucket. The hosts themselves were declared in [NixOS](https://nixos.org/), down to transparent-huge-page settings and TCP buffer ceilings, and the application was shipped onto them with [Kamal](https://kamal-deploy.org/).

Every piece of that was boring and well understood, we already ran Elixir in production, and object storage meant durability was somebody else's problem. It worked, and a version of it is still serving the majority of our traffic while we migrate off it. But the shape had a cost we hadn't reckoned with. A warm cache in every region in front of one durable store means that when an artifact is popular, every region fetches its own copy across the internet, and you pay object storage egress once per region rather than once. Cross-region syncing was never a feature we built, it was a bill we received, and it grew with the one thing we wanted to encourage: more people, in more places, hitting the cache.

We already knew the distance to the data was the problem. Putting a warm copy on every node was a way of not having to move the data, how well a design like that works is the odds that the artifact you want is already on the node you reached. Those odds were worse than we assumed. On the module cache, local disk served 48% of reads, so the other half went to the bucket and more than one request in two for our largest artifacts paid the full trip to object storage while the node acted as an expensive pass-through. The Xcode CAS did far better at 97%, its artifacts being small enough that a useful working set stays resident. Either way, a miss is not something the application can do anything about. The time goes somewhere we don't operate, and the only lever we had was a bigger disk.

## What we got wrong

**We treated resources as unbounded.** The service allocated what it needed, when it needed it, and under real load it needed more than the machine had, so it ran out and got restarted. Our response was to schedule the work instead of letting it happen whenever, which is how eviction, orphan detection, and bucket transfers all ended up as Oban workers. That made the timing of the work predictable. It did not bound the work, and it did not bound anything happening on the request path, which is where the memory went. You can still read the workaround in the host config, where we kept swap around as an OOM shock absorber for transient memory spikes. Making a problem land more evenly is not the same as capping it, and we spent a long time on the first thing while believing we were doing the second.

**Nothing bounded what any one tenant could take.** That is the same missing bound, one flight up. These were multi-tenant hosts, and nothing stopped a single account from taking as much of a node as it could ask for. One team pushing a large module cache upload could saturate a node's bandwidth, and every other account whose build happened to land on that node felt it as a cache that had gone mysteriously slow. There are traces of us fighting this at the edges, like capping how many bytes nginx will hand to `sendfile` in one call so that a single large transfer can't monopolise a worker between small requests. That is a real fix for a real symptom, and it's also a thermostat taped to one wall of a building with no insulation.

**We couldn't simulate the infrastructure locally.** This is the one that made everything else harder to fix. The development setup was one cache node and an nginx in front of it, which is enough to check that a request returns the right bytes and no help at all for the questions that mattered. What happens when a region is unreachable. What a node does when it comes up cold. How a rollout behaves when half the fleet is on the new version. Every one of those had to be answered in production, on real traffic, which meant we answered them slowly and cautiously and often after the fact.

**The deployment was two systems stacked on one box.** The operating system layer was managed by NixOS and the application layer was shipped as containers by Kamal, which is two deployment systems with two different mental models stacked on the same box. Application changes were fine, they rolled warm. Anything in the OS layer, kernel parameters, disk configuration, the nginx build itself, meant taking the server out of rotation, and taking a warm cache node out of rotation isn't a graceful operation. You lose the warmth. The node comes back cold and refills from the bucket, and the people building against that region pay for the deploy.

**And underneath all of it, cloud VMs.** Having spent months worrying about our own tenants competing for a node, we were ourselves a tenant on somebody's hypervisor, sharing hardware with workloads we couldn't see and couldn't influence. Disk latency and network throughput moved around for reasons that had nothing to do with us, which is a miserable substrate for a service whose entire product is predictable tail latency, and it made every performance investigation start with the question of whether the number in front of us was even ours.

## Kura

All five come down to the same gap. Nowhere in that system was there a place to say how much memory, bandwidth, disk, or regional presence a given account should get. Resources were whatever happened to be left over at the moment you asked, and whatever happens to be left over is not something you can reason about, place deliberately, or put a price on.

What we wanted instead was a floor for every account, an amount of the resource that is theirs and that nobody else's traffic can take from them, and a ceiling, so an account in the middle of a burst can't take the node with it. Those resources sit close to where that account's builds run, which makes placement a decision we get to make, where for years it had been a leftover from whichever regions we happened to provision. And nodes reach each other directly rather than meeting in a bucket, because a durable store sitting in the middle of the sync path is a cost that grows with the number of places you serve from. We either absorb that or we pass it on, and passing it on means charging a team more for having a well-factored project and offices in two countries, which is the behaviour we want to encourage. It also decides who gets to use any of this. A per-byte cost that follows every artifact around sets a minimum we have to charge before we break even, and that minimum is what makes a cheap plan impossible. Cheap plans are the whole reason a solo developer or a five-person studio can have a build cache at all, and we didn't want an architecture that priced them out without our ever deciding to.

What that describes is a decentralized cache, so that is what we built. It's written in Rust, we named it [Kura](https://github.com/tuist/tuist/tree/main/kura) after 蔵, the Japanese word for a storehouse, and it's open source under the AGPL v3. Nearly all of the design falls out of a single swap: **local disk is the source of truth, peers replicate to each other, and object storage leaves the hot path entirely.** The replication between them is leaderless: every node is the writer for its own clients and accepts a write without asking anyone's permission first, which is the only arrangement where a build in one region doesn't pay for a round trip to another before its upload is acknowledged. It runs to about seven times the code of the service it replaces, which is roughly what it costs to own the whole data path yourself instead of renting most of it from nginx and a bucket.

## Inside Kura

We picked Rust because of what the service is. A cache node is a data plane whose entire product is predictable tail latency, and memory is the resource that decides whether it keeps that promise. Everything we'd committed to depends on knowing who owns which bytes and when they're released: reserving a response's buffers before opening a reader, attaching a permit to the body and reclaiming it when the last transport owner drops it, mapping a file into a response without copying it, keeping page cache warm for what's being read and releasing the rest. Those are all statements about lifetimes and allocation. A runtime that manages memory on your behalf makes them awkward to express and impossible to enforce, and we'd already lived that. The old service's characteristic failure was allocating more than the machine had and getting restarted for it. We always knew where the memory went. What we couldn't do was hold a byte budget anywhere in the design. Not having a garbage collector matters here for one narrow reason: **a collection pause is a tail latency event, and tail latency is the product.**

Durable state splits into two planes. [RocksDB](https://rocksdb.org/) holds the metadata: manifests, key-value payloads, multipart upload state, namespace tombstones, segment lifecycle records, and the replication outbox. Append-only segment files hold the artifact bodies, and the read path opens their file descriptors directly and never goes through RocksDB for bytes. That puts the small-and-large split into the storage layer itself: **metadata operations and byte operations don't share a code path, so tuning one can't pessimize the other.** Segments are reclaimed by unlinking them. Truncate a file that has live memory mappings and you take the process down with a bus error.

Those segment files are arranged as a ring, and eviction works a whole segment at a time. A node claims half its disk by default and never more than 80% of it, divides that into fixed-size segments, and keeps them in three bands: new segments being appended to, current ones, and an old band that is a fifth of the ring. When space is needed **the oldest segment is unlinked whole, which is one filesystem operation for however many artifacts happened to live in it,** with no per-artifact bookkeeping at eviction time. The old band is what stops this being plain FIFO. A blob read while it sits in that band earns a copy-forward into a fresh segment, which makes it young again, so the ordinary eviction policy needs no special case, nothing has to be remembered or honoured later, and the promotion survives a restart. The size of that band is how much of the working set the cache gets to remember, and a fifth is where we landed.

In memory, a node runs several caches against one shared budget. There's RocksDB's block cache and its write buffers, a byte-bounded cache of manifests, a snapshot cache for the action-cache views, an existence cache, and a cache of open file descriptors for immutable segments so a hot file isn't reopened on every read. What they get sized against matters more than any of them individually. The obvious move is to size them from the memory limit. But the limit is where the kernel kills you. The request is the memory you're guaranteed. On an instance whose limit is several times its request, caches sized from the limit consume the entire guarantee and leave nothing for the buffers a response needs, and **what you get is a node that reports itself healthy and then refuses every request that arrives.** The caches are fitted to the guarantee at startup, shrunk proportionally with a minimum each until they leave the working budget its share, and the node logs what it had to shrink.

Replication falls out of the write itself. A local write commits its metadata and its outbox entry in the same RocksDB batch, so replication intent becomes durable at the instant the write is acknowledged, and there's no window where a node has accepted something it hasn't yet promised to share. The replication is leaderless and eventually consistent, conflicts resolved by last writer wins, so there's no leader, no global lock, and no synchronous fan-out on the hot path waiting for a peer in another region to confirm something. **Replication used to mean that both nodes happened to read the same bucket.**

When a node is new, or has been away, it walks each peer's index newest-first and fetches only the bodies it's missing, keeping a durable watermark per peer that only advances when a pass completes, so a restart picks up where it left off. This is the answer to the old "wait for it to warm up", and **it's the difference between a new region being useful in minutes and being useful next week.** A node joins, finds a peer nearby that already has the working set, and pulls it across a short link instead of assembling it one cold artifact at a time from the other side of the planet.

For the large artifacts, the job is to get the application out of the way of the bytes and then to stop wasting bytes you've already sent. A plaintext artifact read that passes authorization and resolves to a local file gets served with `splice` or `sendfile` on the same port, **without the bytes entering the application at all,** and everything else falls through to the normal path. The old architecture couldn't do that. It needs one process to own both the authorization decision and the file, and we had those in two processes talking to each other over a subrequest. HTTP/2 flow control windows are set explicitly and deliberately left non-adaptive, because without a large enough window a single upload gets throttled to roughly the window divided by the round trip time, and the adaptive implementation ramps a stream up from a small starting window, which halves single-stream throughput when the round trip is long. That decision exists entirely for the developer on a slow link. Reads are resumable too, with every artifact response advertising byte ranges so a client whose download dies asks for the tail instead of starting again. Without it, a transfer that fails at 90% burned 90% of its bytes for nothing, and its retry offers the same load a second time, inflating demand at the moment the link is most congested. Replication yields to client traffic on its own, scaled by how many requests are in flight and by recent latency, so peer sync uses the whole pipe while a node is quiet and gets out of the way when people are using it.

For the small artifacts, once the data is already local, the only enemy left is how many times you have to ask. The action cache can serve a snapshot of an entire namespace in one response: the whole key-to-value map, deduplicated, with a watermark attached. A cold client primes every association it needs in a single round trip instead of thousands, then pulls content through ordinary batched reads, and later requests ask only for what changed since their watermark. There's also a small protocol extension for clients that can't know their output paths ahead of time, which is where the Xcode cache plugin sits, letting them collapse the lookup and the fetch into one round trip instead of two. Both of those are protocol work, and that's the part worth generalizing. Past a certain point, **small-artifact latency stops being an infrastructure problem and turns into a protocol design problem,** and no amount of putting the bytes closer will fix a protocol that asks a thousand questions.

Memory is admitted up front. Every response stream, upload, and materialized response reserves what it needs before it starts, and a read that can't get a reservation is shed with a 429 and a `Retry-After` instead of being allowed to push the node toward getting killed for running out of memory. One rule fell out of that and it's been a good one to hold: a 5xx on the public cache routes means a genuine server fault. **Busy has its own status code.** And there's no shared fleet. Each tenant gets its own mesh of nodes in the regions where their builds run, which gives isolation and means a node's disk holds one team's working set and not everyone's tail.

How we test it answers the mistake that made all the others slow to fix. The local stack is three nodes standing in for three regions, with Prometheus, Loki, Tempo and Grafana alongside them, and the end-to-end suite is seventeen spec files that run against it on a laptop. They cover the questions we previously could only answer in production: what backfill does when a node joins cold, how discovery behaves when peers vanish, what the node sheds under forced memory pressure, whether a large artifact survives a handoff between nodes, whether peer mTLS refuses the wrong certificate, and what a rollout does halfway through. A separate harness runs the previous release, then the current one, then the previous release again over the same volumes, which is how we check that a fleet running mixed versions mid-deploy stays coherent. **Every one of those used to be a question we asked production and waited to find out.**


## Moving to Kubernetes

All of that describes one node. A fleet of them is a different problem: one mesh per account, spread across the regions that account builds from, each sized to what it stores, with instances appearing and retiring as traffic moves. That is the problem that took us to Kubernetes, which is a large enough change that it deserves its own post and will get one. Floors, ceilings, placement and retirement are all scheduling, and we had been trying to express scheduling in two tools with no vocabulary for it.

Placement follows an account's traffic. A primary instance relocates when another region has durably carried the majority of that account's runs. A paid account expands into a second region once that region's own traffic clears an absolute threshold, and deliberately not a share of the total, because a large primary would otherwise veto every other site forever: a busy second office deserves a local instance even while the first one still dominates the mix. A secondary retires when its traffic falls under a much lower threshold, and **the gap between those two numbers is what stops an instance flickering in and out of existence.** The windows are calendar spans. Nobody builds seven days a week, and a rule that broke its streak every weekend would never fire for a normal team.

What an account is entitled to shapes where it can be at all. An account that has named a storage region is served from that region and never from another, whatever it pays, because the instance holds those artifacts. Accounts on the free tier run in a default region unless they've asked for Europe. Paid accounts constrained to a country group resolve into that group's pool, and paid accounts with no constraint resolve in a fixed order: an explicit assignment if an operator made one, then whichever region their live instance is already in so that resolution never relocates a running account, then the default.

An account's disk claim is sized from evidence, and never from a quota. When a node's ring fills it sheds its oldest segments and reports the shed age, which is how soon after being written an artifact was lost to size pressure, and that number is what drives an account's disk claim upward. Occupancy drives it back down, because a ring that is too large never evicts and therefore produces no shed age at all to learn from. The worse the shedding, the shorter the confirmation window before the claim grows, so an account being actively hurt gets an answer in hours, not weeks.

Those claims are what made Kubernetes worth it. Every cache pod requests its claim's worth of ephemeral storage, so the scheduler bin-packs instances against each node's real disk and declines to place one that doesn't fit. That's exact, it's per node, and it can't drift from what the cluster holds. What it replaced was a forecast computed from a quota table checked into our own repository, **which by the time we deleted it was wrong by roughly a factor of three.** Under NixOS and Kamal the shape of the fleet lived in configuration files and in our heads, and the only way to find out whether something fit was to put it there and watch. Now the cluster is the authority on itself, and questions like whether a region can take another instance have answers we can query instead of estimate.

There is a limit to how close we can put a node, though, and for some teams the right place isn't a region we operate at all. Plenty of our customers run their own CI machines, in their own datacenter or under their own office, and nothing we host is going to beat a cache sitting on the same network as the builders. So Kura nodes can be self-hosted and still belong to the mesh. A customer runs one next to their own infrastructure, it enrolls with our control plane and heartbeats to stay a member, and from then on it replicates with the managed nodes over an authenticated peer connection like any other. The placement question doesn't go away, it gets an answer we could never have given on our own, **which is that the customer makes the call.**

Rolling a new version across that fleet is its own piece of durable control-plane state, advanced one reconciler tick at a time. A progressive rollout moves through account-grouped waves with a health gate between them, and it can pause itself, be aborted, or be superseded when a newer tag arrives. Wave zero is our own accounts. Waves one through three order everyone else by recent usage ascending, so **the accounts absorbing the earliest exposure to a new version are the ones with the least traffic riding on it.** The grouping is by account and not by node, deliberately: an account's mesh spans regions and can include self-hosted peers, and splitting one mesh across waves would hold cross-version skew open inside it for the length of the whole rollout instead of a single wave. Servers provisioned while a rollout is in flight come up on the tag the fleet started from until their own account's wave lands, so a version paused as suspect never reaches a fresh node. There's an expedited mode that fans out all at once, which is what canary and staging use and what we reach for when rolling back to a version we already trust.

## The numbers

At the median, a request's time to first byte is 0.5ms, against 9.4ms on the service it's replacing, so roughly seventeen times faster for the request a build makes most often. At the tail, gRPC requests come back within 3.8ms at the 99th percentile where the old service's 99th percentile was 2.1 seconds, though the two aren't serving identical traffic mixes and the old number includes registry reads that go out to object storage, so the median is the fairer comparison to lean on. Over seven days the fleet served 12.2 million artifact reads, took 3.3 million writes, and moved 9.2 TB of egress, which is around 1.3 TB a day, and an artifact lives about 6.6 days on a node before eviction reclaims the space.

And the honest part: **Kura is carrying roughly an eighth of our cache request volume today. The migration is still in flight.** What it has been carrying is the hardest end of the traffic, which is the part we most needed the design to survive, and it has.

## Rolling it out

Which cache a client talks to is decided by the client. The CLI sends a feature-flag header with every request, the server reads it, and the answer to "where does my cache live" comes back as Kura endpoints for a client advertising `KURA` and as the old fleet's endpoints for one that isn't. Until recently you had to set that flag yourself, and [now the CLI sends it by default](https://github.com/tuist/tuist/pull/12760), which means **a team moves onto Kura the next time they update the CLI, and not at a moment we picked for them.** That hands the pace of the rollout to something we don't control. Upgrades arrive in whatever order teams are comfortable with, exposure ramps on its own, and a version that turns out to be wrong is only ever held by as many people as the release reached. The flag decides more than routing, too. It also selects the shape on the machine, whether Xcode's compilation cache goes through the machine-wide CAS proxy and plugin or the older per-project daemon, so one switch moves both ends of the path together and no client ends up half migrated. Setting `TUIST_FEATURE_FLAG_KURA=0` puts a machine straight back on the old path, with no deploy on our side and no ticket to us.

An account arriving for the first time doesn't have an instance waiting for it. The request asking where its cache lives is the same signal that provisions one, and while that's happening the answer it gets is the old fleet, carrying a `Cache-Control` of thirty seconds so the client comes back for the real answer shortly instead of holding a stand-in for the hour a settled answer is good for. **Nobody builds against a cold cache during the handover, because the thing they were already using is the fallback.** All of which is why the number above says an eighth and not all of it.

So the old service stays up. For as long as there are CLIs out there that don't advertise the flag, they need somewhere to go, and that means running both and paying for both for a while yet. The step after this one moves the default from the client to the server, so an account routes through Kura whatever version it happens to be running and the flag becomes an opt-out rather than an opt-in. **Only after that does retiring the old service become a question with an answer.** We're in no hurry. The old service works, it has carried the majority of our traffic through this entire migration, and keeping it running costs us a good deal less than pulling it out from under somebody's build.

## What it cost us

We treated build system caching as a storage problem. Storage problems have excellent off-the-shelf answers, and that's what makes the framing dangerous, because object storage answers the storage question so well that it stops you noticing the question changed underneath you when your artifacts got small. The second thing is less comfortable to write. Every one of those five mistakes announced itself early, as a restart, a slow build somebody else's upload had caused, a deploy that took a region cold. We treated each one as a defect and fixed it where it surfaced, and every fix worked, which is what kept us from noticing they were one problem wearing five costumes. In March we shipped a distributed key-value layer to buy replication inside the shape we already had, and reverted it eleven days later. That was the design telling us in plain language, and the months we spent treating symptoms before we listened are the real cost here, more than any of the individual decisions that led there.

We came out of this with three things and not one: Kura itself, the infrastructure it runs on, and the seam between them, where questions we used to answer by guessing became questions the cluster answers about itself. We'd have undersold that third one in November. It's what makes the rest worth the seven-times-the-code price, because a cache you can place deliberately and size from evidence is a cache whose economics you can write down. **Once you can write the economics down, you can offer the same cache at the same speed to a solo developer and to a company running thousands of builds a day.** Paired with [Once](https://github.com/tuist/once), our attempt at letting a project connect the automation it already has to caching and remote execution without migrating to a new build system first, that's the bridge we're after: a team keeps the build system and the scripts it already has, and its builds and test runs get to fly on infrastructure nobody there had to assemble.
