In 1905, a Hamburg America Line steamer crossed from Hamburg to New York in nine days. A [Hapag-Lloyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hapag-Lloyd) container ship on the same route today is scheduled at eleven days and eighteen hours. A request from a laptop in Berlin to a server in Virginia resolves in about 350 ms. That is still a long way from instant, and further still from the same request between two machines on one network, which comes back in under a millisecond. Colocation, they call it.

Build caches sit on that same ladder. It took us some time to internalize that a build system with a remote cache is only as good as two things: the build graph that decides what can be skipped, and the latency of asking the cache whether it already has the answer. You're trading one ceiling for another. Instead of being bound by how many cores you have and how fast they run, you're bound by the latency and the bandwidth of the network between you and the cache. Shortening that latency while keeping the bandwidth high is the whole problem.

Build systems are not the only technology that has had to fight physics to make something feel fast. In 1995, Tim Berners-Lee, then at MIT, saw that the web was going to choke on its own congestion and challenged his colleagues to find a better way to deliver content. Tom Leighton, an applied mathematics professor with an office down the hall, took it up with a graduate student named Danny Lewin, and what came out of it was [consistent hashing](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/258533.258660): a way to spread content across a shifting set of servers without reshuffling all of it every time one joins or leaves. They founded [Akamai](https://www.akamai.com/company/company-history) on it in 1998, and the diagnosis behind it is the one we arrived at nearly thirty years later. The bottleneck was never the origin server. It was the distance between the origin and whoever was waiting.

The closer we got to solving this for build systems that deal in very small binary artifacts, like Xcode or Bazel's, the more obvious the flaws in the system we'd designed became. That sent us back to the drawing board to sketch what a design would have to look like to carry the request volume and the bandwidth those build systems demanded. Two things came out of it. One was a move to Kubernetes and our own runners, where we can colocate cache and compute. The other was a piece of technology we called Kura, after 蔵, the Japanese word for a storehouse. This is the story of what got us here, and how Kura came to be.

## It started with the module cache

The [module cache](https://tuist.dev/en/docs/guides/features/cache/module-cache) came out of [project generation](https://tuist.dev/en/docs/guides/features/projects). Because Tuist generates the Xcode project, it knows the dependency graph, and knowing the graph is what lets you work out which modules haven't changed, swap them for precompiled binaries, and generate a project that skips compiling them entirely. What comes out the other side is a particular kind of artifact: whole compiled modules, frameworks, the output of real compilation work. Across five million module cache downloads, the median artifact a client pulls sits between 256 KB and 512 KB, a tenth of them are above 4 MB, one in a hundred is above 32 MB, and the last fraction of a percent runs past 256 MB. Which meant that from the first day our performance profile was bandwidth-bound. One lookup, then a large transfer, and the round trip barely registering against the size of what we were moving.

So we put them in object storage, which is what most teams reach for and what most teams should reach for. It's cheap, it's durable, **it's somebody else's operational problem**, and it moves large objects perfectly well. For a long stretch it did what we needed. What we hadn't priced in was what happens to a managed object store when you point real build traffic at it, and we found out faster than we expected. At our volumes of uploads and downloads, every provider we tried started producing failures, and because those failures happened between the client and the bucket, they surfaced to developers as network errors in the middle of a build with nothing on our side to point at and nothing we could do to fix them. We switched providers. It happened again. Being unable to answer "why did my build fail" with anything better than a shrug is not a position you can stay in, and once we'd seen the same shape of problem from more than one vendor it stopped looking like a bad provider and started looking like the architecture.

The physics were pushing in the same direction. The assumption that comes bundled with reaching for object storage, usually without anyone stopping to examine it, is that it will keep being fine for any artifact size. It won't, and we wrote up the reasoning in [The physics of build systems](/blog/2026/08/25/the-physics-of-build-systems): a cache hit costs roughly `R × L + S / B`, where R is the number of round trips you can't overlap, L is latency, S is the bytes you have to move, and B is the bandwidth you have to move them through. Which term dominates depends entirely on the shape of your artifacts. Large ones put you squarely in `S / B`, where object storage does fine and the distance to the bucket is an implementation detail. Small ones put you in `R × L`, where the distance is the entire cost and no amount of cheap durable storage will save you. None of this made our original decision wrong. It was a correct answer to the workload we had, but **the workload underneath us changed.**

## Then the artifacts got small

Xcode 26 introduced compilation caching built on LLVM's content-addressable storage, at sub-function granularity, and [we plugged into it](/blog/2025/10/22/xcode-cache) as soon as it shipped. It is a completely different shape of traffic arriving at the same door. Not one transfer of a framework but thousands of tiny lookups, a great many of them doing nothing more interesting than asking whether something exists. The median artifact the Xcode CAS serves is between 8 KB and 16 KB, and its 99th percentile is under a megabyte, which is roughly where the module cache's distribution begins. It also arrives four times as often: twenty-one million of those lookups against five million module cache downloads over the same period. Same infrastructure, inverted profile. Every property that made object storage a good fit for the module cache stopped mattering, and the one property we'd never had to care about, the distance to the data, became the only one that did. The split is easiest to see on the fleet still carrying most of our traffic, where both workloads run side by side on the same nodes. The Xcode CAS accounts for **80% of the requests and 6% of the bytes**. The module cache accounts for 20% of the requests and 94% of the bytes, at an average of 3.8 MB a download against the CAS's 60 KB. Almost every request is small and almost every byte is in a large one, and the same system has to be good at both.

[Bazel](https://bazel.build/) pushes in the same direction. A remote execution cache is thousands of small action lookups and blob fetches, so the operation count climbs and the artifact size falls again, and everything we'd learned about being bandwidth-bound stops applying. That is also where the economics started to come apart. Object storage bills you per operation as well as per byte, and a handful of operations costs nothing, but a build doing tens of thousands of PUTs and GETs is a different invoice entirely. The part that made it hard to plan around is that the operation count isn't ours to control. It falls out of how a team has structured their project: a finely grained graph with many small modules produces far more and far smaller artifacts than a coarse one, so two accounts on the same plan could land different costs on us, and the account with the better-factored project was the expensive one. Reliability we couldn't explain, physics moving against us, and a cost curve driven by somebody else's dependency graph. It was clear we had to do something.

## The cache in front of the cache

Our first answer, built between November 2025 and the spring, was a cache service written in [Elixir](https://elixir-lang.org/). It ran as a [Phoenix](https://www.phoenixframework.org/) application, one instance per host, on ten cloud VMs spread across eight regions. The shape was a warm cache in front of durable storage: each node had a volume mounted at `/cas`, [nginx](https://nginx.org/) sat in front and served artifact bytes off that volume through a zero-copy `sendfile` path with kernel TLS, and object storage held the authoritative copy. Phoenix never touched the bytes on a read. It answered an `auth_request` subrequest, handed nginx an `X-Accel-Redirect`, and got out of the way. Each node tracked artifact access metadata in [SQLite](https://sqlite.org/) so eviction could be least-recently-used rather than random, [Cachex](https://github.com/whitfin/cachex) held the key-value store and auth results, and a fleet of [Oban](https://github.com/oban-bg/oban) workers handled eviction, orphan cleanup, and batched transfers to and from the bucket. The hosts themselves were declared in [NixOS](https://nixos.org/), down to transparent-huge-page settings and TCP buffer ceilings, and the application was shipped onto them with [Kamal](https://kamal-deploy.org/).

Every piece of that was boring and well understood, we already ran Elixir in production, and object storage meant durability was somebody else's problem. It worked, and a version of it is still serving the majority of our traffic while we migrate off it. But the shape had a cost we hadn't reckoned with. A warm cache in every region in front of one durable store means that when an artifact is popular, every region fetches its own copy across the internet, and you pay object storage egress once per region rather than once. We never chose that. It came with the shape, and it got more expensive as people used the cache from more regions, which was the growth we wanted.

We already knew the distance to the data was the problem. Putting a warm copy on every node was a way of not having to move the data, how well a design like that works is the odds that the artifact you want is already on the node you reached. Those odds were worse than we assumed. On the module cache, local disk served 48% of reads, so the other half went to the bucket and more than one request in two for our largest artifacts paid the full trip to object storage while the node acted as an expensive pass-through. The Xcode CAS did far better at 97%, its artifacts being small enough that a useful working set stays resident. Either way, a miss is not something the application can do anything about. The time goes somewhere we don't operate, and the only lever we had was a bigger disk.

## What we got wrong

I remember standing in the airport in Chicago with [Marek](https://github.com/fortmarek), waiting on a flight home from [Deep Dish Swift](https://deepdishswift.com/), and the two of us agreeing that something was wrong and that we had to do something about it. If we didn't, this was going to stop being an engineering problem and start being a business one. That was the _prompt_ we needed. Five things came out of it, and they took us longer to see than they should have.

**We treated resources as unbounded.** The service allocated what it needed, when it needed it, and under real load it needed more than the machine had, so it ran out and got restarted. Our response was to schedule the work instead of letting it happen whenever, which is how eviction, orphan detection, and bucket transfers all ended up as Oban workers. That made the timing of the work predictable. It did not bound the work, and it did not bound anything happening on the request path, which is where the memory went. You can still read the workaround in the host config, where we kept swap around as an OOM shock absorber for transient memory spikes. Making a problem land more evenly is not the same as capping it, and we spent a long time on the first thing while believing we were doing the second.

**Nothing bounded what any one tenant could take.** That is the same missing bound, one flight up. These were multi-tenant hosts, and nothing stopped a single account from taking as much of a node as it could ask for. One team pushing a large module cache upload could saturate a node's bandwidth, and every other account whose build happened to land on that node felt it as a cache that had gone mysteriously slow. There are traces of us fighting this at the edges, like capping how many bytes nginx will hand to `sendfile` in one call so that a single large transfer can't monopolise a worker between small requests. That is a real fix for a real symptom, and it's also a thermostat taped to one wall of a building with no insulation.

**We couldn't simulate the infrastructure locally.** This is the one that made everything else harder to fix. The development setup was one cache node and an nginx in front of it, which is enough to check that a request returns the right bytes and no help at all for the questions that mattered. What happens when a region is unreachable. What a node does when it comes up cold. How a rollout behaves when half the fleet is on the new version. Every one of those had to be answered in production, on real traffic, which meant we answered them slowly and cautiously and often after the fact.

**The deployment was two systems stacked on one box.** The operating system layer was managed by NixOS and the application layer was shipped as containers by Kamal, which is two deployment systems with two different mental models stacked on the same box. Application changes were fine, they rolled warm. Anything in the OS layer, kernel parameters, disk configuration, the nginx build itself, meant taking the server out of rotation, and taking a warm cache node out of rotation isn't a graceful operation. You lose the warmth. The node comes back cold and refills from the bucket, and the people building against that region pay for the deploy.

**And underneath all of it, cloud VMs.** Having spent months worrying about our own tenants competing for a node, we were ourselves a tenant on somebody's hypervisor, sharing hardware with workloads we couldn't see and couldn't influence. Disk latency and network throughput moved around for reasons that had nothing to do with us, which is a miserable substrate for a service whose entire product is predictable tail latency, and it made every performance investigation start with the question of whether the number in front of us was even ours.

## Kura

All five come down to the same gap. **Nowhere in that system was there a place to say how much memory, bandwidth, disk, or regional presence a given account should get.** Resources were whatever happened to be left over at the moment you asked, and whatever happens to be left over is not something you can reason about, place deliberately, or put a price on.

What we wanted instead was a floor for every account, an amount of the resource that is theirs and that nobody else's traffic can take from them, and a ceiling, so an account in the middle of a burst can't take the node with it. Those resources sit close to where that account's builds run, which makes placement a decision we get to make, where for years it had been a leftover from whichever regions we happened to provision. And nodes reach each other directly rather than meeting in a bucket, because a durable store sitting in the middle of the sync path is a cost that grows with the number of places you serve from. We either absorb that or we pass it on, and passing it on means charging a team more for having a well-factored project and offices in two countries, which is the behaviour we want to encourage. It also decides who gets to use any of this. A per-byte cost that follows every artifact around sets a minimum we have to charge before we break even, and that minimum is what makes a cheap plan impossible. Cheap plans are the whole reason a solo developer or a five-person studio can have a build cache at all, and we didn't want an architecture that priced them out without our ever deciding to.

What that describes is a decentralized cache, so that is what we built. It's written in Rust, we named it [Kura](https://github.com/tuist/tuist/tree/main/kura) after 蔵, the Japanese word for a storehouse, and it's open source under the AGPL v3. Nearly all of the design falls out of a single swap: **local disk is the source of truth, peers converge directly with one another, and object storage leaves the hot path entirely.** The replication between them is leaderless: every node is the writer for its own clients and accepts a write without asking anyone's permission first, which is the only arrangement where a build in one region doesn't pay for a round trip to another before its upload is acknowledged. It runs to about seven times the code of the service it replaces, which is roughly what it costs to own the whole data path yourself instead of renting most of it from nginx and a bucket. We didn't arrive at any of this from nothing. We took a lot from [Buildbarn](https://github.com/buildbarn/bb-storage), in particular the way its blobstore composes mirrored, read-fallback and sharded backends instead of treating storage as one undifferentiated thing.

## Inside Kura

Everything below is one node on one machine. The fleet comes after.

### Why Rust

The first thing on that list of five was memory we never bounded, so it is the thing the rewrite had to answer first. A cache node is a data plane whose entire product is predictable tail latency, and memory is the resource that decides whether it keeps that promise. Everything we'd committed to depends on knowing who owns which bytes and when they're released: reserving a response's buffers before opening a reader, attaching a permit to the body and reclaiming it when the last transport owner drops it, mapping a file into a response without copying it, keeping page cache warm for what's being read and releasing the rest. Those are all statements about lifetimes and allocation, and a runtime that manages memory on your behalf makes them awkward to express and impossible to enforce. We always knew where the memory went in the old service. What we could never do was hold a byte budget anywhere in the design. Not having a garbage collector matters here for one narrow reason: **a collection pause is a tail latency event, and tail latency is the product.**

### Where the bytes live

That settles who owns a byte while it is in memory. On disk the question is a different one, which is what deserves to live next to what. [RocksDB](https://rocksdb.org/) holds the metadata: manifests, key-value payloads, multipart upload state, namespace tombstones, segment lifecycle records, the action-cache index, and the replication outbox. Append-only segment files hold the artifact bodies, and the read path opens their file descriptors directly rather than pulling bytes back through RocksDB. The smallest bodies are the exception: action-cache entries and small objects are stored inline in RocksDB, where giving them a file of their own would cost more than the bytes themselves. That puts the small-and-large split into the storage layer itself: **metadata operations and byte operations don't share a code path, so tuning one can't pessimize the other.** Segments are reclaimed by unlinking them. Truncate a file that has live memory mappings and you take the process down with a bus error.

Those segment files are arranged as a ring, and eviction works a whole segment at a time. A node claims half its disk by default and never more than 80% of it, divides that into fixed-size segments, and keeps them in three bands: new segments being appended to, current ones, and an old band that is a fifth of the ring. When space is needed **the oldest segment is unlinked whole, which is one filesystem operation for however many artifacts happened to live in it,** with no per-artifact bookkeeping at eviction time. The old band is what stops this being plain FIFO. A blob read while it sits in that band earns a copy-forward into a fresh segment, which makes it young again, so the ordinary eviction policy needs no special case, nothing has to be remembered or honoured later, and the promotion survives a restart. The size of that band is how much of the working set the cache gets to remember, and a fifth is where we landed.

### One shared memory budget

Serving out of those segments costs memory, and it is not the only thing asking. A node runs several caches against one shared budget. There's RocksDB's block cache and its write buffers, a byte-bounded cache of manifests, a snapshot cache for the action-cache views, an existence cache, and a cache of open file descriptors for immutable segments so a hot file isn't reopened on every read. The obvious move is to size them from the memory limit. But the limit is where the kernel kills you. The request is the memory you're guaranteed. On an instance whose limit is several times its request, caches sized from the limit consume the entire guarantee and leave nothing for the buffers a response needs, and **what you get is a node that reports itself healthy and then refuses every request that arrives.** The caches are fitted to the guarantee at startup, shrunk proportionally with a minimum each until they leave the working budget its share, and the node logs what it had to shrink.

Memory is admitted up front. Every response stream, upload, and materialized response reserves what it needs before it starts, and a read that can't get a reservation is shed with a 429 and a `Retry-After` instead of being allowed to push the node toward getting killed for running out of memory. One rule fell out of that and it's been a good one to hold: a 5xx on the public cache routes means a genuine server fault. **Busy has its own status code.** And there's no shared fleet. Each tenant gets its own mesh of nodes in the regions where their builds run, which gives isolation and means a node's disk holds one team's working set and not everyone's tail.

### The two sizes of fast

That keeps a node alive under load. Making it quick is two problems rather than one, because the small artifacts and the large ones want opposite things. For the large ones, the job is to get the application out of the way of the bytes and then to stop wasting bytes you've already sent. A plaintext artifact read that passes authorization and resolves to a local file gets served with `splice` or `sendfile` on the same port, **without the bytes entering the application at all,** and everything else falls through to the normal path. The old architecture couldn't do that: it needs one process to own both the authorization decision and the file, and we had those in two processes talking over a subrequest.

HTTP/2 flow control windows are set explicitly and left non-adaptive. The adaptive implementation ramps a stream up from a small starting window, which at 100ms of round trip measured 9.84 MB/s against 20.65 MB/s with a fixed one. That decision exists entirely for the developer on a slow link.

Reads are resumable, with every artifact response advertising byte ranges, so a client whose download dies asks for the tail instead of starting again. Without it a transfer that fails at 90% burned 90% of its bytes for nothing, and its retry offers the same load a second time, at the moment the link is most congested.

For the small artifacts, once the data is already local, the only enemy left is how many times you have to ask. The action cache can serve a snapshot of an entire namespace in one response: the whole key-to-value map, deduplicated, with a watermark attached. A cold client primes every association it needs in a single round trip instead of thousands, then pulls content through ordinary batched reads, and later requests ask only for what changed since their watermark. There's also a small protocol extension for clients that can't know their output paths ahead of time, which is where the Xcode cache plugin sits, letting them collapse the lookup and the fetch into one round trip instead of two. Both of those are protocol work, and that's the part worth generalizing. Past a certain point, **small-artifact latency stops being an infrastructure problem and turns into a protocol design problem,** and no amount of putting the bytes closer will fix a protocol that asks a thousand questions.

## Going global

Until this point we've looked at Kura as a single instance, but many of the teams we work with are spread out: Mac machines warming the cache in `us-east`, a development team in Berlin 🍺 running Gradle or Bazel compilations. So we designed for distribution from the start, without a durable S3 layer sitting in the middle of the sync path, which adds both unreliability and cost. Supporting that, and having a way to express it, is what motivated the move to Kubernetes, a change large enough that it deserves its own post and will get one. Floors, ceilings, placement and retirement are all scheduling, and we had been trying to express scheduling in two tools with no vocabulary for it.

### Replication and cold starts

Each node reads a bounded feed from every peer and asks for what it's missing, which puts the work of spreading a write on the nodes that want it. A commit writes one feed row however many peers there are, and because the receiver is the one asking, it knows what it already has and can refuse a blob before any bytes move.

Between regions, one gateway per region does the exchanging, so bytes cross a region boundary once instead of once per node. Replication is leaderless and last writer wins, and every node accepts its own clients' writes without waiting for anyone.

When a node is new, or has been away, it walks each peer's index newest-first and fetches only the bodies it's missing, keeping a durable watermark per peer that only advances when a pass completes, so a restart picks up where it left off. This is the answer to the old "wait for it to warm up", and **it's the difference between a new region being useful in minutes and being useful next week.** A node joins, finds a peer nearby that already has the working set, and pulls it across a short link instead of assembling it one cold artifact at a time from the other side of the planet.

Replication yields to client traffic on its own, scaled by how many requests are in flight and by recent latency, so peer sync uses the whole pipe while a node is quiet and gets out of the way when people are using it.

### Where a node goes

All of that gives an account a mesh of its own, with bounded resources and nodes that keep each other current, but it says nothing about where those nodes should be. Which regions does an account get, when does one get taken away, and how do the floors and ceilings move?

**The first instance goes wherever there's room**, because on day one there's no traffic to place it from. If that guess is wrong, and four runs in five turn out to come from somewhere else, it gets corrected inside a fortnight.

After that, a region that has carried the majority of an account's runs for two weeks takes the primary, and no more than once a quarter, so a team spread across two timezones doesn't get dragged back and forth. A region that clears a floor of its own gets an instance as well, 25 runs a day on Pro or 100 on Air, where the plan funds fewer of them so each one has to earn more. That floor is an absolute number and not a share of the total, because a share would let one big office veto a local instance for every other site a company has. Fall under a much lower floor, 5 a day on Pro, and the instance is retired. The plan caps how many an account holds at once: two on Air, three on Pro, five on Enterprise.

Each window counts calendar days, so a quiet weekend doesn't reset anything. Nobody builds seven days a week, and a rule that needed an unbroken streak would never fire for a normal team.

### Running the fleet

How much disk should an account get? We used to answer that with a table checked into our own repository, and by the time we deleted it the numbers in it were wrong by about a factor of three. Now the nodes answer it instead. When a node's ring fills up it drops its oldest segment and reports how old that segment was, which is a direct measure of how long an artifact survives before size pressure kills it. If artifacts are dying young, the account needs more disk. If the ring never evicts at all it is too big, and we take some back. The worse the shedding, the less time we wait before believing it, so an account that is actively losing hits gets more disk in hours rather than at the end of the month.

Versions roll out the same way, slowly and mostly on their own. A rollout moves through waves of accounts with a health check between each one, and it can stop itself, be cancelled, or be replaced when a newer build shows up. Wave zero is us. After that, accounts go in order of recent traffic, smallest first, so the people trying a new version on everyone else's behalf are the ones with the least riding on it. A server provisioned halfway through a rollout boots on the old tag and waits for its account's turn, which means a version we have paused as suspect never lands on a fresh node. There is also a mode that skips all of this and ships everywhere at once. That one is for canary, for staging, and for going back to something we already trust.

A wave always takes whole accounts, never individual nodes, and that rule exists because a mesh is not necessarily ours. Plenty of our customers run their own CI machines, in their own datacenter or in a rack under the office, and we are never going to beat a cache sitting on the same switch as the builders. So a Kura node can be self-hosted and still belong to the mesh. It enrolls with our control plane, heartbeats to stay a member, replicates with the managed nodes over an authenticated connection like any other, and upgrades when its account's wave comes round. Now imagine splitting that mesh across two waves: the customer's own hardware on one version and ours on another, for as long as the whole rollout takes. Nobody wants to debug that. The placement question does not disappear for these teams, it just gets an answer we could never have worked out ourselves, which is whatever the customer decided.

### Next to the runners

Colocation is the oldest trick in this business. Put the cache on the same switch as the builders and the distance to the data stops being interesting. The catch is that you have to own the builders, which is why it tends to be the answer from companies selling compute, and why it does nothing at all for someone on a laptop.

Then we started selling compute. [Tuist Runners](https://tuist.dev/en/docs/guides/features/runners) run on our hardware, and the obvious move was to park a cache beside them and call it done.

We gave them an ordinary Kura node instead. The same reconciler that decides whether you can dispatch a job decides whether you get one, so it arrives with your runners, leaves with them, and needs no configuration in either direction. It also peers with the rest of your mesh like every other node, which means the artifacts a CI machine warms never stay in the datacenter that warmed them. A commit builds on our hardware in one region, and the next person to open that branch on a laptop somewhere else finds the objects already nearby.
