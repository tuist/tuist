There was a time I used to go to run, and it was the perfect time to give podcasts a listen. I had started a business with Marek, and my interests gravitated towards stories from people that had done a similar transition or I respected the way they ran companies. One of those days, I gave a listen to a podcast interview to Tobi, the CEO of Shopify, the company that had employed me until I started the entrepreneurial journey. Some of the points he touched on, I was familiar one, but in this one there was a piece that kept me thinking until today, and it’s a piece where he talks about an inflection point at the company where he decided to engineer the company. I witnessed results of that work, one of which was a unique compensation system that gave employees control over their compensation. As an engineer, my natural reaction was to wonder how we’d engineer Tuist if we designed the company the way we’d like it to be engineered.

At the time we did what made the most sense, and what most founders do. Buy most of the solutions that are not core to the business. One solution for support here, another one for CRM there, and this tool here for errors and observability... We build the system buying tools that felt right and were sound price wise. However, something felt wrong. I’m a very system thinker, and all I saw was loose dependencies across different services, with entities schemas duplicated across service, and layers of indirection moving state around to keep things in sync. This mess is the consequence of this multi-SaaS model with specialized solutions for every domain, but we’d rather have a simpler model. It felt right, from the perspective of we can’t afford building anything ourselves, but felt wrong from the perspective of how we’d operate as a company.

At the same time, if you’ve never built a company, you wonder how to stay relevant within an ocean of companies heavily invested that can throw money at everything they can imagine. People don’t know about us? We’ll throw money at the largest booth in every conference we can imagine. We have some money left? Then we can pay some people to talk about us. It was a bit intimidating cause we had far less money. We also tried to see if there was anything it was worth to learn from them, but it felt by the book. It felt we could anticipate what would come out of them next. They felt very predictable.

Despite being a David in front of a Goliath, we noticed a small detail that would connect so beautiful with the idea of engineering the company, and with something that was happening around the same time, the emergence of coding harnesses and the shift in how we develop. Throughout this observation, we had noticed an interesting pattern. These organizations were heavy hierarchical-wise, and if they were not, they were on the path of becoming one, because they were racing more money, and that usually come with the cost of promising that you’ll hire specialized roles to hopefully get you closer to the next round. Sadly things don’t work that way. While people might play a role, whether an idea will thrive and unlock a new revenue growth for you, or not, is usually random, so you can easily find yourself juggling managing all that many people, and the processes around that, and the communication between them, and your quest to meet the expectations we gave to investors.

We were 4 people at the time, the cost of building something something was going down, so we thought, what if we engineer the organization around efficiency, such that we can deliver more value quicker, with a good balance with product quality and a level of care we always strived to have, like the one I’m putting in this blog post so people have something to draw inspiration from beyond a bunch of “absolutely” put together, own most of the context of the organization, and build an efficient system around it, heavily leaning on agents, and automating anything that’s automatizable and bringing us (humans) where we can bring the most humans. It wasn’t a let’s replace everything with AI, but more let’s go domain by domain, understanding how we’d like to work on each of them, then model that in a centralized place, which we ended up naming Atlas (turns out many other people adopted the same name letter, but we don’t care). Ideas became DB tables, and the migrations that made them possible, dashboard UI, MCP tools, and a business logic design that aligned with how we’d like to work on each of those areas.

This is not something we’d have considered before LLMs cause it was simply not possible cost-wise. Shopify in fact was heavily invested in building their own in-house tools, cause they could afford it, and more so now that they make heavy usage of AI tools tool. And this, weirdly creates a crazy momentum, where the more features you bring, and start wiring them together and making the work the way you’d like to work, it has a magnetic effect towards moving features over. What used to be scattered pieces of context, with in cases not the right APIs to access it cause it’s not in the interest of the SaaS to add them or might do just charging you a lot, suddenly you find yourself with data data, and not just data, but with edges of pieces of information being strong database relationships all forming a context graph of your organization. I have never been part of a transition from a micro-service architecture to a monolith one, but this is how I imagine it must have felt. You go from your organizational context being a distributed mesh with walls and webhooks with the best effort to keep all the information in sync, with everything being together in a single place and endless possibilities. It’s like being given a white canvas, and a lot of imagination, and let the oil colors flow through the movements in your hand and the brush. I don’t care if people say if this is not focusing on our main business. The focus never went away from there, but if we wanted to build a business with the freedom of not having many incentives to align, this is the investment we had to do. For context, I do many of the operations beyond product development, and I do everything through Codex or Claude cause they have access to all our system of context. I’m not the best at each domain, but I get to learn a lot, and have a better understanding of where a person can bring a lot of value, vs where we could just keep going with what we have. I know... if you have money, you could just skip that step, and hire a person per domain by looking at the record of past jobs, but... it’s funnier to get one’s hands dirtier, and engineer the organization, like Tobi mentioned in the podcast. We were engineering the organization the way we saw ourselves working, while observing all our competitors raising more rounds, and hiring specialized roles, and seeing the products completely stagnated. I won’t like to you, the slow pace of development of our competitors is something I’m always intrigued about, cause they are 20x the number of people we are, and one might expect that’s directly correlated with the work that one ships, but also experience working with many organizations is that it’s the processes themselves what actually make those 20x number of people not bring the returns one expects. How come did some companies took so long to see that organizations might start moving back to GitHub Actions and therefore they’ll have to package their offering as just runners to bring there? Or how come do organizations put case studies of customers they don’t have or with so many quotes that there are more quotes than non-quotes? I don’t know... it’s still amazing to me, but what I learned over these years, is that the level of care is something that organizations might trade early on, perhaps cause there’s a strong obsession for making money and growing? I’m a firm believer the care is what brings the other, even if it’s slow, cause it’s a long-term bet.

Back to Atlas, cause I diverged a lot from it. I already wrote about it in the past, at the time I just saw it as a tool to improve our sales process, but it has since grown into becoming our operations platform that spans all the domains of the company, and I thought it’d be a good time to do a 2.0 extension of that blog post, with everything else that landed since, how we approached each domain, and the role that AI played in it. I’ll skip the part of “we’ve done everything with coding agents” cause I think it’s something we can take for granted these days, but I’d just say that this investment builds on previous investments that we’ve done and that we are grateful we did. The design system, Noora, that powers the Tuist dashboard, Elixir, which we became very fluent working with at development and production, and k8s, which would serve as a foundation for us to declare and get on production this new system. From there, one necessary piece is obviously having the ability to map solutions to products or product features. Many people talk these days about developers becoming product or project managers, and I couldn’t agree more with that. This is something I was doing before this trend happened, and it’s in fact why I loved so much coding agents and refused to jump into conversations about yeah, it doesn’t write code as well as me. Suddenly I could do things that were exclusive to large organizations, like building a build system. Everyone at the org has the same level of care of curiosity that was also a key ingredient to invest into Atlas and believe this is going to pay great dividends. 

One aside note. Each of those tools that we used is great, so I won’t mention any of them. The issue wasn’t each tools looked individually. The issue was the complex system that emerges on top of building a mesh of proprietary services whose APIs and design we can’t influence. And at Tuist we like simplicity, by a lot. Turns out, from most of the services we only use 4% of the things that we offered, which in LLM terms, is a few prompts of work. Turns out that the investment would also come with not having to manage authorization across many different services, which you know... we could do through Okta, but we’d end up in the enterprise plan of many organizations, just to have better control there. It’d have killed the company. And I won’t talk about the 1-SaaS-1-agent, embedded in a website or in Slack model. Isn’t it better just having a bot that has access to all the context instead of bots that have access to siloed context? Less is more.

Let’s talk about sales.

## Sales

Developers don’t like to be sold. They like to have a bureaucracy-free process to test the product. They don’t buy bullshit. They smell it from the distance. They build trust if they feel the people on the other side are experts of the thing they are selling, and not just charlatans, which if I’m not mistaken it’s xxx in the english speaking world. That means for us we skip the traditional outreaches, putting something on ProductHunt, spamming communities on Reddit, or paying every newsletter to show up at the top. Instead we focus on doing great craft. and talking about it, like I’m doing in this blog post. Care and depth for craft, is something contagious and something you feel you want to support. Note this doesn’t work with everyone. Some people might feel you are not as smart as you think you are, and can go crazy distances pushing against you, but this is a thing everywhere. The point that I was trying to get to is that we invest in people showing up here, and once they show interest, we need a system to keep track of everything that’s happening with that organization. People use prospects, and then use leads and prospects for various phases. I hate the terminology. We have people or organizations with interest in solving one of their needs with our products. Punto. So what we need is a system to keep track of how their tests are evolving, a bit like an archeology of their interaction with the product so we can guide them towards the results they expect. So we built a sales sections and made it very centric, trying to auto capture as much context as possible automatically, for example through Slack, email conversations, and meeting transcripts, while making it copy-paste-image in cases where the context was not easily capturable. The annoying part of multi-services, remember?

So this makes it possible on one side, have conversations backed by data. I recently added a relationship between organizations and POCs, which have a public page read only that teams can use to get an overview of everything that has happened from the beginning of the POC, including all the context that we gathered from the first call. The page is customized to the customer... Cause details matter. Additionally, we wired them with their usage in the platform, so we can get an overview which features they are using or not. And this, stored over time, creates a room for another solution we landed recently. We call it Nudges, and it’s inspired by a call we had with Grafana’s sales representative.

![Public Atlas proof of concept brief for Buildify's evaluation of Tuist, with context, success criteria, scope, and timeline](/marketing/images/blog/2026/09/23/atlas-what-we-built-next/buildify-poc-brief.png)

Tyler suggested to jump on a call to help us optimize our costs in Grafana and take the opportunity to talk about how they could support us best. We left the call thinking, wow, this is the level of sales experience we should provide to our organizations. So with all the data in placed we build nudges, which is a touch point suggestion based on usage signals that we believe are a good moment to have a conversation with them, for example if their cache effectiveness has gone done suspiciously, or they stopped using a feature all of a sudden. This is how you show someone you care about them and build a system around caring at scale. And here, is where a human needs to have those conversations, and where it’ll make sense to loop more people, most likely engineers themselves, and support them with this system. Everything is integrated on Slack, so we get the nudge notifications with suggestions to act on it. We started doing this kind of follow ups, and it’s crazy how effective they are. 

Accounts also include the service leves agreed in service agreements, so we can run an incident against all of them and get back to them if there’s any vioularuon. Orgs are also links to contacts with their roles, so we know who to write and when, the documents scoped to the organization, and a timeline of events, which range from manual notes, to auto capture events from meetings or written exchanges with them.

A lot of this is common sense. Trying to automate everything is miopic. Anything fake will make developers allergic, but it might work with the leadership, specially if they are so detached from the problem and the solution space, that they can make decisions without really understanding if it's the best one for their organization. Surprisingly, this is how many sales work these days, not the way we'd like them to happen. We want both, leadership and developers' passion for the product to meet.

## Finances

From there we moved to understanding our finances. We don't have infinite money, so understanding where the money is going is crucial. We integrated with our banks, which to our surprise, they expose an API, so we can sync all the transactions, and then use agents to classify the transactions as they show up in our system. This classification makes it possible to understand our cost structure, spot opportunities to cut costs, and also understand understand things that, when you are small, it's even more crucial, like your runway, how much money you burn per month, and also do projections so you can understand where your next investments shold go.

Atlas is completely headless, which means everything that's visible or doable through the dashboard, is also accessible by agents, so most of operations these days are run directly from Claude or Codex, the harnesses we use the most at the team. So our understanding or decision making usually start with a conversation with those harnesses, which know how to go to Atlas to have a conversation with us about it. Also, this is all wired with Slack through a bot, so we can have the conversations there, drawing inspiration from Shopify's River, and we also send notification system. In the case of Finances, we have a weekly pulse that tells us if things are going well or if we are drifting in a direction that's not positive for the company.

## Support

We have 3 support channels. The first one is the community Slack forum and issues on repositories. Since the repositories are open source, people create issues there, and just recently, we added notification system from GitHub to Slack so we could stay on top of those requests coming from those channels. The community forum is also a place where requests happen, and we monitor all of them actively. Then there's Slack connect channels, which we setup with enterprise custoemrs, and it's a very direct and fast report system. We monitor anything our enterprise customers bring up, and try to act on them right away, skipping any kind of "I'll create an issue and I'll get back to you later". We are at a phase where we can afford something like that, and adding a ticketing system feels really innecessary where it could just be a prompt and a deployment. We never liked the obscurity of I created a ticket and I'll get back to you. The last channel, which is usually the one people and orgs that show up use, is email and/or in-browser message box. We were using before one of those Intercom-like tools that unify the inbox, and exposes various relays to it, like an in-browser chat box, or email inboxes, so we copied the whole idea, and merged it into Atlas, and made things that wouldn't have been possible otherwise. One of them, is binding support threads with existing accounts, so you can follow the relationship and get access to all the context tha tmight be relevant to drive that conversation forward. The other one is having an MCP to operate with those support items to do things like resolving them, or responding to them. As I mentioned earlier, our usage of Atlas is very headless, and the graphical interface is Claude or Codex, so it's very common to start our days asking the agent to navigate the support items, one at a time, and discuss how to act on them. The cool thing of this is that I can do it on the go cause I have the iOS app connected to the same MCP too. I can also lean on agents to make suggestions like, this is spam, this is what I'd do, so agents are a bit of a first-line triage. I love it. Additionally cause we control the experience, Asmit designed the whole in-browser component that people use to communicate with us, using our design system and components. Isn't that amazing?

## Email

We don't send many emails beyond transactional ones from the main application, for example to confirm the authorship of an email address, but sometimes we do. For example if we need to notify customers that have been impacted by an incident, or tell on-premise customers that they need to update to a version, or to people that just showed interested in receiving updates. We don't send many of the latter cause we find them annoying so we try to do something brief, personal, and straight to the point without trying to make a bunch of flashy announcements that you probably don't care about. Guess what, we were using a solution for this, so we moved it over. As part of the execution we had two audiences, static and dynamic ones. Although now giving it some thought, maybe the name is not that accurate. In one, addresses are manually added, either from our side or the users themsleves, who sign up through an online form. The other one is dynamically generated based ons ome criteria (e.g. all the customers that are enterprise, or all the customers that are self-hosting Tuist). And... since we don't have data in two systems, Atlas can directly get that data merging the data from Atlas and Tuist itsel. There's no syncing through webhooks system that can get out of date easily. Not a good system design :). This makes it possible to iterate on a digest email with Claude and dend send it as an MCP tool call. Or just tell enterprise customers impacted by an incident with all the context about the incident in a conversation with Claude. There's no copying and pasting code around, it goes straight from the session to the people's inbox, and I think this is beautiful.

## Engineering

You might have seen a lot of people talking about software factories, and many organizations gravitating towards becoming one, trying to achieve this ultimate dream of I have a bunch of agents coordinating and deliverying software in a very autonomous matter. I'm not sure that'll be the case for everything. While some of the work could definitively be automated end to end, like acting on an alert of a slow query coming from Grafana, or a small issue reported on GitHub, when it comes to executing new or large features, then we remain very human-driven, running sessions locally, and eventually, making them outlive the environment that originated them using our own compute environments. So instead of trying to have a factory kind of model inside Atlas, we instead focused on bringing the data upon which we could automate some of those engineering operations.

The first one was errrors. We use the Sentry SDKs in our apps, so we complied with their server-side protocol, and made the database schema changes in ClickHouse to persist errors there and have an alerting system on Slack replicating everything that we had before. We took the opportunity to then add features that are only possible when you have data proximity, like connecting errors with potential enterprise customers impacted by the errors, so we not only can use that information to prioritize the work on errors, but also to notify the customer that we've resolved something that they faced.

For larger pieces of work, we use specs, which we use as internal mechanism to align of a major decision. The format is very simple, just a body, comments from the team, and a state. The good thing of this is tahtw e have embeds, so we can in the future easily go back here and find the reason why we do something in a particular way.

Postmortems are internal, with a user-facing public page that we can share with them so they can see in detail everything that happened, how we ended up there, how we detected it, the timeline, and the action items we are taking to prevent it from happening again and detecting it earlier if it happens

You might have noticed we don't have the concept of tasks. We were using in fact at ask management solution, with everything that comes with it, like milestones, projects... you name it. But since most of the requests become a prompt, which morphs into a PR whcih we merge and release/ship, we don't find the need to build a system to move items around. We are aware that this will have to evolve at some point, specially as the throughput of request increases, but we see it more as a queue, where things are prioritized based on the type of request and the originator of the request, but I think all the overhead of figuring out a planning system around it is necessary. A queue makes more sense in this world.

A lot of them, so instead of using something like Google Drive to store them, we built a documents section in Atlas, using S3 as a durable storage, and topped it up with embeds and classification and title assignemtns using agents, so we can do more sophisticaded searches. ## LLM gateway

While working on agentic features into the system, it became obvious we needed a solution that would where our token credits would go. So we build an LLM gateway. The usage and cost would be bound to what we call profiles. For example we have a "Translation" profile, which is used for all the translation operations, and another one for "Ops" inside Atlas. Then within each profile, we can generate tokens, and each of them has the usage bound too. Everyone continues to use the coding subscription locally, which works quite well for everyone, and here we use open weight models through inference providers, adding credits as needed, while ensuring they are used in the most efficient way possible.

## closing

But Pedro, how does a working day look for you? I spend a lot of day with Claude and Codex with both connected to Atlas. As mentioned earlier, my usage of Atlas is very headless. I have sessions that through through all the support tickets, while in parallel I create a spec to discuss something with the team, or work on an error that has been reported to Atlas. People talk a lot about software factories, but software lives in a broader context that expands beyond just software, so I think when it comes to getting the most of the technology, is that whatever system one comes up with, should expand beyond software. An error has value, and an error in the context of who affects has even more value, and if that's connected with a service level agreement, even more. You get the idea? It's not something you can't build on a diversity of solution, but remember, distributing a solution across sytems you don't control, and whose interface comes with a bound contract and the incentives of the company behind it is something you might want to avoid long-term, and the cost of preventing that is very minimal these days.

You might wonder, how long it took us to build something like this, and my honest answer is not much. Just yesterday I built the public POC page feature, in just a few prompts. I added the hardware section in one afternoon a couple of weeks ago. We owe Elixir, Noora, our design system, and our investment in Kubernetes a lot. The source code is available at [tuist/tuist](https://github.com/tuist/tuist/tree/main/atlas), but it's coupled to our way of working, and we don't have plans in the near future to turn it into a product, although I'd like to you if I told you I don't have a bit of an appetite for that. I met some friends in Barcelona who recently sold their company, and when I showed them how much we had automated through Atlas their reaction was, "wow, that could be a business on its own". A lot of things are changing, and I believe it'll require a lot of year of playing with the ideas, for models to emerge,a nd potentially turn that into some generic open source solution that anyone can use. Like leaving wine in bottles for many year. Our focus is users of Tuist, and Atlas is an enabler for us to provide better service, faster, and with just a team of four people. We made it open source because we think the world benefits from more secret sauce being shared, not otherwise. Pretty much everything of how we operate and build technology is in the open these days, cause as one of our competitors pitched us, no "open source is not a trend", open source is a lifestyle and a formula we believe can inspire and empower others to build better business, and if we can do that while building Tuist, even better.

There are stil many pieces that live outside of Atlas, and that we won't replace for obvious reasons. We won't build payment infrastructure cause we have Stripe, or Slack, which we use as our communication tool among ourselves and with customers and the community. Or a solution for signing documents. And there are others that while we don't see replacing immediately, I have a hunch we'll get to them. Once of them is Grafana, which we run through their hosted version, cause it monitors a cluster that might go down, so it makes sense for the whole piece to be external, although more and more, our usage is entirely headless through their MCP and also the alerting system. The other piece is Git hosting. I was chatting the other day with a company that has already moved their Git solution to their internal solution inspired by [Cursor's blog post](https://cursor.com/blog/git-at-any-scale), and my reation with this was, it makes total sense. When I think of our usage of GitHub, it's very basic, and has evolved a lot since we started using agents more and more. One thing is clear, cause everything is so fluid, we'll design everything to have minimal ties with external services, and that includes specific solutions build upon models vertically integrated, unless the value is huge, cause we think layers are going to commoditize fast.
